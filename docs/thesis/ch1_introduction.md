## 1.1 Background

During major floods, people are stranded on rooftops, cling to floating objects or wade through water, often in places that rescue teams cannot see from the ground. Typhoon Yagi and the floods and landslides that followed in September 2024 left 321 people dead and 24 missing in Vietnam, according to the Viet Nam Disaster and Dyke Management Authority as of 23 October 2024 [@unrco2024yagi]. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) can sweep a large area in a short time and are already used in search and rescue. A single flight, however, produces tens of minutes of 4K video, while the operator has to fly the aircraft, watch a small screen outdoors and report positions to the rescue team at the same time. People in the video are also very small: at a safe flight altitude, a person covers only a few tens of pixels in a frame of almost nine million pixels. Missing one person means missing one life.

This thesis builds a system that **automatically detects people in UAV video, estimates their GPS position and sends alerts to a ground station**, running directly on the embedded computer carried by the UAV. Instead of reviewing the whole video, the rescue team receives the places where people were seen on a map, together with a search area and an image crop for confirmation.

## 1.2 Three Constraints

Three constraints of the problem drive every design choice in the following chapters.

**People are very small in the image.** With a camera of 82° diagonal field of view (the wide-angle camera of the DJI Matrice 4T) and 4K video (3840 × 2160), a person about 0.5 m wide covers the number of pixels shown in Table 1.1. Once the body is under water and only the head (about 0.2 m) remains visible, the target is even smaller.

**Table 1.1.** Size of a person in the image by flight altitude; 82° diagonal camera, 3840 px wide video, nadir view (`runs/gsd/m4t_diag82/gsd_table.csv`).

| Altitude | Ground footprint width | Ground sampling distance | Person (0.5 m) | Head (0.2 m) | Person after resizing to 640 px |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 m | 45.46 m | 1.184 cm | 42.24 px | 16.89 px | 7.04 px |
| 50 m | 75.76 m | 1.973 cm | 25.34 px | 10.14 px | 4.22 px |
| 80 m | 121.22 m | 3.157 cm | 15.84 px | 6.34 px | 2.64 px |
| 100 m | 151.53 m | 3.946 cm | 12.67 px | 5.07 px | 2.11 px |

Common object detectors such as YOLO resize the image to 640 px before processing. For 4K video this step divides the image size by six, and a person seen from 100 m shrinks to about 2 px (last column), below what any model can detect. The system therefore has to keep the native resolution, either with a larger input size or by cutting the image into tiles.

**The edge device is weak.** Embedded computers that a UAV can carry (for example the NVIDIA Jetson family) run at a few tens of watts or less. They cannot run a detector on 30 frames of 4K video per second; in practice they process only a few frames per second. The question is whether a few frames per second are enough. A person stays inside the camera footprint for a time that depends on altitude and ground speed: at 50 m and 10 m/s this is about 4.3 seconds, so the person is seen at most 8 times when 2 frames are processed per second (`docs/TECHNICAL.md` §2.4). Because the detector finds the person in each view only with a certain probability, the number of views decides whether the system can confirm the person at all.

**The network is unreliable.** Flooded areas often lose power and mobile coverage, and the link from the UAV to the ground station may drop packets or disappear for tens of seconds. Streaming video to the ground for processing is not feasible. The system must process on board, send only small alert messages, store them while the link is down and resend them when it returns, without losing any alert.

## 1.3 Research Questions

The thesis answers three research questions from the proposal **[CHECK AGAINST PROPOSAL: copy the exact wording]**:

**Research question 1 (accuracy).** How accurately can small people be detected in UAV images of flooded areas, and how much do individual techniques (input resolution, tiling, fine-tuning, a P2 detection head, altitude-based frame downscaling) help, in particular for people smaller than 16 px?

**Research question 2 (speed on the edge device).** Which configuration reaches the required processing rate (about 2 frames per second) while keeping the highest recall?

**Research question 3 (system).** When detections are combined over several frames for confirmation and alerts are sent over an unreliable network, how many false alarms and misses does the system produce, how long does an alert take to reach the ground station, and which confirmation method tolerates GPS error and camera motion?

Each question is broken down into measurable sub-questions (Table 1.2).

**Table 1.2.** Sub-questions and how they are answered.

| Code | Sub-question | Answered by |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1a | How small can a person be before being missed? | Recall by person-size group; recall as a function of pixels on the person |
| RQ1b | Which training data help: urban UAV images, people in water, disaster images with pasted people? | Comparing fine-tuned models on the same evaluation sets |
| RQ1c | How much do tiling and a P2 detection head help small people? | With and without tiling; with and without the P2 head |
| RQ1d | How much does altitude-based frame downscaling help? | Tiling with and without downscaling on 4K images with real flight altitude |
| RQ1e | How much do flood-specific nuisances (glare, floating debris, blur, weather) degrade the detector? | Recall by nuisance type on the flood test set |
| RQ1f | How well does a model trained on urban and maritime images transfer to flood scenes? | Comparing the same models on urban, maritime and flood images |
| RQ2a | How much faster is a compressed model (FP16, INT8), and how much accuracy does it lose? | Measurement on a GPU with TensorRT |
| RQ2b | Which configurations are not dominated by another in both speed and accuracy? | Recall-versus-speed trade-off table |
| RQ2c | Can a weak device sustain the required rate, and how much does altitude-based downscaling help? | Device timing simulation calibrated from measurements |
| RQ3a | How many processed frames per second are enough, and how does this change with altitude and speed? | Flight simulation |
| RQ3b | Is grouping on ground coordinates better than image-space tracking when few frames are processed? | Real video with telemetry and simulation |
| RQ3c | How far do GPS, altitude and heading errors shift the alert position? | Error-propagation model and simulation |
| RQ3d | How much do link outages delay alerts, and is any alert lost? | Network simulation |
| RQ3e | How many duplicate alerts does merging alerts into incidents remove? | Merging at the ground station on real video |

The three questions share one **cross-cutting argument**: the flight data a UAV already has (altitude, GPS, heading) helps at all three stages. It chooses the image scale for detection (RQ1d), reduces the computation so that a weak device keeps a usable rate (RQ2c), and confirms people when only a few frames per second are processed (RQ3b). This is not a fourth question but the thread connecting the three; Chapter 7, Section 7.4 brings it together.

## 1.4 Contributions

1. **A UAV flood test set and a size-aware evaluation protocol.** No public dataset of real UAV flood imagery with person labels exists. The thesis labels 188 frames (509 people) from 9 video sources, checks them for duplicates against all training data and locks them with a cryptographic hash before any model is run. All results are broken down by person size (below 8, 8–16, 16–32 and above 32 px), with confidence intervals obtained by resampling whole videos. The small size of the set is stated as a limitation.
2. **Altitude-based frame downscaling inside an edge pipeline.** Resizing images according to flight altitude appears in earlier work (Chapter 2). The thesis places this step in a pipeline driven by per-frame telemetry and measures two effects at once on a detector fine-tuned for small people: accuracy by person size, and the number of tiles a weak device has to process. It is evaluated on 4K images with real flight altitude (SeaDronesSee) and in simulation; public flood videos carry no altitude, so it cannot be evaluated on the flood test set. 3. **Ground-plane confirmation at low processing rates.** The thesis shows why a common multi-object tracker (ByteTrack) breaks when the UAV flies fast and only a few frames per second are processed, why compensating camera motion with telemetry helps only when GPS is very stable between two frames, and confirms a person before alerting by grouping detections by their position on the ground. Onboard flight parameters have been used for multi-object tracking from drones (Chapter 2); the thesis analyses and measures it at low processing rates for search and rescue, against image-space association with and without motion compensation.

Beyond these contributions, the thesis implements a **complete system** from video to alerts on a map: detection, geolocation, confirmation, an outage-tolerant send queue, a ground station that merges and prioritises incidents, and tools for the rescue team. It also provides the flight, network and edge-device timing simulators used for evaluation. Chapters 3 and 5 describe its requirements, design, implementation and quality assurance. The source code is open and covered by automated tests.

## 1.5 Scope and Limitations

- The target is **people** in colour (RGB) images taken from a UAV looking almost straight down. Thermal imagery, posture and the urgency of each person are out of scope.
- There is no real flight over a flood and no Jetson device. Detection is evaluated on labelled images (urban, maritime and the self-labelled flood set); speed is measured on a T4 GPU and compared only in relative terms, while weak devices are estimated; confirmation, geolocation and alert delivery are evaluated on maritime video with real telemetry and in simulation. Every simulated or estimated result is labelled as such.
- Most training experiments were run once (one seed) because of the GPU budget.
